Visualising Solid Shapes

Chapter 10 · Grade 8 Mathematics

Moving from 2D drawings to understanding true 3D forms — how shapes look, how we count their parts, and how we represent them on paper.

Topics covered: 3D shape review · Views of solids · Euler's formula · Maps vs pictures · Polyhedra and Platonic solids

3D Shapes: A Review

Common 3D Shapes

Cube: 6 square faces, all equal. Example: dice.

Cuboid (Rectangular Prism): 6 rectangular faces. Example: brick, matchbox.

Cylinder: 2 circular flat faces + 1 curved surface. Example: tin can.

Cone: 1 circular base + 1 curved surface tapering to a point. Example: ice-cream cone.

Sphere: Perfectly round, no edges or vertices. Example: ball.

Prisms and Pyramids

Prism: Two identical, parallel polygonal bases joined by rectangles.

  • Triangular prism: triangular bases
  • Square prism = cuboid
  • Hexagonal prism: hexagonal bases

Pyramid: One polygonal base; all other faces are triangles meeting at an apex.

  • Square pyramid: square base + 4 triangular faces
  • Triangular pyramid = tetrahedron
Number of faces in a prism = n + 2 (where n = sides of base polygon). In a pyramid = n + 1.

Views of 3D Shapes: Front, Side, and Top

A 3D solid can look completely different depending on the direction from which you view it. Engineers and architects use three standard views.

Three Standard Views

Front view (elevation): What you see looking straight at the front of the object.

Side view (side elevation): What you see looking from the left or right.

Top view (plan): What you see looking directly from above (bird's-eye view).

Example: Cylinder

Front view → Rectangle

Side view → Rectangle

Top view → Circle

Example: Cone

Front view → Triangle

Side view → Triangle

Top view → Circle with a dot at centre

Example: Sphere

Front / Side / Top view → All circles!

Example: Cube

Front view → Square

Side view → Square

Top view → Square

Isometric drawing represents 3D objects on a 2D surface using a triangular dot grid, preserving relative dimensions.

Euler's Formula: V − E + F = 2

For any convex polyhedron, the number of Vertices (V), Edges (E), and Faces (F) are always related by:

V − E + F = 2

Verification with Common Shapes

Cube: V = 8, E = 12, F = 6

8 − 12 + 6 = 2 ✓

Triangular Prism: V = 6, E = 9, F = 5

6 − 9 + 5 = 2 ✓

Square Pyramid: V = 5, E = 8, F = 5

5 − 8 + 5 = 2 ✓

Tetrahedron: V = 4, E = 6, F = 4

4 − 6 + 4 = 2 ✓

Using Euler's Formula

If a polyhedron has 12 faces and 30 edges, how many vertices?

V − 30 + 12 = 2

V = 2 + 30 − 12 = 20

This is the dodecahedron (12 pentagonal faces)!

Important Limitations

  • Formula applies only to polyhedra (flat faces, straight edges)
  • Does not apply to shapes with holes (e.g., a doughnut)
  • Does not apply to non-convex (concave) polyhedra in all cases
Leonhard Euler (1707–1783) discovered this relationship — one of the most elegant results in geometry!

Maps vs Pictures; Scale in Maps

Maps vs Photographs/Pictures

Photographs/Pictures:

  • Show perspective — objects closer appear bigger
  • Not to a fixed scale; dimensions not directly readable
  • Represent a scene from a particular viewpoint

Maps:

  • Top-down (bird's-eye) view
  • Drawn to a fixed scale throughout
  • Distances can be measured and converted
  • Direction is consistent (North usually up)

Scale in Maps

Scale expresses the ratio of map distance to actual distance.

Scale = Map Distance / Actual Distance

Example: A map has scale 1 : 50,000. If two towns are 4 cm apart on the map:

Actual distance = 4 × 50,000 = 200,000 cm = 2 km

Another example: Scale 1 cm : 5 m. A room is 3 m × 4 m. On the map: 0.6 cm × 0.8 cm

Maps use a uniform scale — every part of the map uses the same ratio, unlike pictures which show perspective distortion.

Polyhedra: Convex, Concave, and Platonic Solids

Types of Polyhedra

Polyhedron: A 3D solid with flat polygonal faces, straight edges, and sharp vertices. No curved surfaces.

Convex polyhedron: Any two points inside can be joined by a segment that stays entirely inside. No "dent." Example: cube.

Concave (non-convex) polyhedron: Has at least one "dent" — a segment joining two interior points may go outside. Example: a star-shaped solid.

Regular polyhedron: All faces are identical regular polygons and all vertices are identical.

The Five Platonic Solids

These are the only 5 convex regular polyhedra:

Tetrahedron: 4 equilateral triangles, V=4, E=6, F=4

Cube (Hexahedron): 6 squares, V=8, E=12, F=6

Octahedron: 8 equilateral triangles, V=6, E=12, F=8

Dodecahedron: 12 pentagons, V=20, E=30, F=12

Icosahedron: 20 equilateral triangles, V=12, E=30, F=20

All five Platonic solids satisfy Euler's formula: V − E + F = 2. Plato associated them with the four elements and the cosmos!

Chapter Summary – Visualising Solid Shapes

Key Concepts

  • 3D shapes have length, breadth, and height; key types include prisms, pyramids, cylinders, cones, spheres
  • Any solid can be viewed from front, side, or top — each gives a 2D projection
  • Euler's formula: V − E + F = 2 holds for all convex polyhedra
  • Maps are to scale (top-view); pictures show perspective
  • Scale = map distance ÷ actual distance
  • Only 5 Platonic (convex regular) solids exist

Key Terms

Polyhedron: Solid with flat faces

Edge: Line segment where two faces meet

Vertex: Corner point where edges meet

Face: Flat polygonal surface

Convex: No "dents"; all diagonals inside

Prism: Two parallel identical bases

Pyramid: One base, triangular lateral faces

Euler's Formula: V − E + F = 2

Scale: Ratio of map distance to real distance
