Squares and Square Roots

Chapter 6 · Grade 8 Mathematics

Squaring a number and finding its square root are inverse operations. Understanding perfect squares and efficient methods to find square roots are fundamental skills in mathematics, used in geometry (Pythagoras theorem), physics, and many other areas.

Perfect Squares and Their Properties

The square of a number n is n × n = n². A perfect square is the square of a whole number (natural number).

Perfect Squares (1 to 20)

  • 1² = 1    2² = 4    3² = 9
  • 4² = 16    5² = 25    6² = 36
  • 7² = 49    8² = 64    9² = 81
  • 10² = 100    11² = 121    12² = 144
  • 13² = 169    14² = 196    15² = 225
  • 16² = 256    17² = 289    18² = 324
  • 19² = 361    20² = 400

Properties of Perfect Squares

  • The square of any integer is always non-negative (never negative).
  • Numbers ending in 2, 3, 7, 8 are never perfect squares.
  • Perfect squares end only in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9.
  • If a number ends in an odd number of zeros, it is NOT a perfect square.
  • The square of an even number is even; the square of an odd number is odd.
  • Between n² and (n+1)² there are exactly 2n non-square natural numbers.
Square of negative = positive: (-n)² = n²

Patterns in Squares and Pythagorean Triplets

Patterns in Perfect Squares

Difference of consecutive squares:

(n+1)² - n² = 2n + 1

Examples: 4 - 1 = 3 (= 2×1+1); 9 - 4 = 5 (= 2×2+1); 16 - 9 = 7 (= 2×3+1)

Sum of first n odd numbers:

1 + 3 + 5 + ... + (2n-1) = n²

Examples: 1+3 = 4 = 2²; 1+3+5 = 9 = 3²; 1+3+5+7 = 16 = 4²

Square of a number ending in 5:

(a5)² = a(a+1) followed by 25

Example: 35² = 3×4 followed by 25 = 1225

Pythagorean Triplets

A set of three positive integers (a, b, c) where a² + b² = c² is called a Pythagorean triplet. c is the hypotenuse of a right triangle.

a² + b² = c²

Generating triplets: For any m > 1:

( 2m, m² - 1, m² + 1 ) is a Pythagorean triplet

Examples:

  • m = 2: (4, 3, 5) — check: 16 + 9 = 25 ✓
  • m = 3: (6, 8, 10) — check: 36 + 64 = 100 ✓
  • m = 4: (8, 15, 17) — check: 64 + 225 = 289 ✓
  • m = 5: (10, 24, 26) ✓

Other well-known triplets: (5, 12, 13); (7, 24, 25); (9, 40, 41).

Square Root by Prime Factorisation

To find the square root of a perfect square using prime factorisation:

  1. Express the number as a product of its prime factors.
  2. Arrange the prime factors in pairs.
  3. Take one factor from each pair.
  4. Multiply the selected factors — the product is the square root.
√(p² × q² × r²) = p × q × r

Example 1: √324

324 = 2 × 162 = 2 × 2 × 81
      = 2² × 3 × 27 = 2² × 3 × 3 × 9
      = 2² × 3² × 3²
√324 = 2 × 3 × 3 = 18

Verify: 18² = 324 ✓

Example 2: √1764

1764 = 2 × 882 = 2 × 2 × 441
      = 2² × 441
441 = 3 × 147 = 3 × 3 × 49
      = 3² × 7²
1764 = 2² × 3² × 7²
√1764 = 2 × 3 × 7 = 42

Verify: 42² = 1764 ✓

Checking if a number is a perfect square: In the prime factorisation, every prime factor must appear an even number of times. If any prime factor appears an odd number of times, it is NOT a perfect square.

Square Root by Long Division Method

The long division method works for any number and is especially useful for large numbers or decimals.

Steps

  1. Group the digits in pairs from the right (units place). The leftmost group may have 1 or 2 digits.
  2. Find the largest integer whose square is ≤ the first group. This is the first digit of the square root. Write it as both the quotient and divisor.
  3. Subtract the square from the first group and bring down the next pair of digits.
  4. Double the current quotient and write it as the new partial divisor (with a blank digit at the end).
  5. Find the largest digit d such that (partial divisor with d appended) × d ≤ current remainder. Write d in the quotient and as the units digit of the divisor.
  6. Subtract and bring down the next pair. Repeat until done.

Example: √529

Pairs: 05 | 29
Step 1: Largest n where n² ≤ 5 is 2 (2²=4). Quotient so far: 2. Subtract: 5-4=1.
Step 2: Bring down 29 → remainder = 129. Double quotient: 2×2=4 → partial divisor: 4_.
Step 3: Find d: 43×3=129 ≤ 129. d=3. Quotient: 23. 129-129=0.
√529 = 23

Verify: 23² = 529 ✓

Square Roots of Decimals and Fractions

Square Roots of Fractions

√(p/q) = √p / √q

Find the square root of numerator and denominator separately.

Example 1: √(25/49)

√(25/49) = √25 / √49 = 5/7

Example 2: √(144/169)

√(144/169) = 12/13

Example 3 (mixed number): √(6 ¼) = √(25/4) = 5/2 = 2.5

Convert mixed numbers to improper fractions first, then apply the rule.

Square Roots of Decimals

Method 1 – Convert to fraction:

√0.04 = √(4/100) = 2/10 = 0.2
√1.44 = √(144/100) = 12/10 = 1.2

Method 2 – Long division for decimals:

Group digits in pairs: to the left of decimal point (right to left) and to the right of decimal point (left to right).

Example: √2.56

Pairs: 02 | .56
Integer part: √2 → 1 (1²=1), remainder = 1
Bring down .56 → 156. Double: 2_.
26×6 = 156. d = 6. Quotient = 1.6
√2.56 = 1.6

Verify: 1.6² = 2.56 ✓

Summary: Squares and Square Roots

Key Formulas

(n+1)² - n² = 2n + 1
Sum of first n odd numbers = n²
Pythagorean triplet: 2m, m²-1, m²+1 (m>1)
a² + b² = c² (right triangle)
√(p/q) = √p / √q

Properties of Perfect Squares

  • End only in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, 9
  • Never end in 2, 3, 7, 8
  • Odd number of trailing zeros = NOT a perfect square
  • Even prime factor exponents = perfect square

Methods for Square Root

Prime Factorisation Method:

  1. Factorise the number into primes
  2. Pair up the prime factors
  3. Take one from each pair and multiply

Long Division Method:

  1. Group digits in pairs from right
  2. Find largest square ≤ first group
  3. Double quotient for new divisor
  4. Find next digit; repeat
