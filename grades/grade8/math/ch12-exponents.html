Exponents and Powers

Chapter 12 · Grade 8 Mathematics

A compact notation for repeated multiplication — and the key to handling the very large and very small numbers found in science.

Topics covered: Laws of exponents · Zero and negative exponents · Standard form (scientific notation) · Comparing large and small numbers · Real-world examples

Laws of Exponents — Part 1

If a is any non-zero number and m, n are integers:

Law 1: Product of Powers

aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ

When multiplying powers with the same base, add the exponents.

Example: 2³ × 2⁴ = 2³⁺⁴ = 2⁷ = 128

Example: x⁵ × x³ = x⁸

Law 2: Quotient of Powers

aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ (a ≠ 0)

When dividing powers with the same base, subtract the exponents.

Example: 3⁶ ÷ 3² = 3⁶⁻² = 3⁴ = 81

Example: y⁷ ÷ y³ = y⁴

Law 3: Power of a Power

(aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ

When raising a power to another power, multiply the exponents.

Example: (2³)⁴ = 2¹² = 4096

Example: (x²)⁵ = x¹⁰

Caution: Different Bases

These laws only apply when the base is the same.

2³ × 3³ ≠ 6⁶

Use Law 4 (next slide) for same exponent, different bases.

Memory trick: Same base + multiply → ADD exponents. Same base + divide → SUBTRACT exponents.

Zero Exponent, Negative Exponents, and Product Law

Zero Exponent

a⁰ = 1 (for any a ≠ 0)

Why? Using Law 2: aⁿ ÷ aⁿ = aⁿ⁻ⁿ = a⁰, and any number divided by itself = 1.

Examples: 5⁰ = 1, 100⁰ = 1, (−3)⁰ = 1

Negative Exponent

a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ (a ≠ 0)

Why? a⁰ ÷ aⁿ = a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ

Examples:

2⁻³ = 1/2³ = 1/8

10⁻⁴ = 1/10000 = 0.0001

5⁻¹ = 1/5 = 0.2

Law 4: Power of a Product

(ab)ⁿ = aⁿ × bⁿ

Distribute the exponent over each factor.

Example: (2 × 3)⁴ = 2⁴ × 3⁴ = 16 × 81 = 1296

Example: (xy)³ = x³y³

Law 5: Power of a Quotient

(a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ (b ≠ 0)

Example: (2/3)⁴ = 16/81

Combining Laws

(2x²y)³ = 2³ × x⁶ × y³ = 8x⁶y³

Key: a⁻ⁿ and 1/aⁿ are reciprocals of each other: aⁿ × a⁻ⁿ = a⁰ = 1

Standard Form (Scientific Notation)

Very large or very small numbers are written compactly as:

M × 10ⁿ where 1 ≤ M < 10 and n is an integer

Converting to Standard Form

Large numbers: Move decimal point left; count moves = positive n

3,450,000 = 3.45 × 10⁶

92,900,000 = 9.29 × 10⁷

1,000,000,000 = 1 × 10⁹

Small numbers: Move decimal point right; count moves = negative n

0.000043 = 4.3 × 10⁻⁵

0.00000001 = 1 × 10⁻⁸

Converting Back to Standard

Positive power → move decimal right:

2.7 × 10⁴ = 27,000

Negative power → move decimal left:

6.02 × 10⁻³ = 0.00602

Operations in Scientific Notation

Multiply: (3 × 10⁴) × (2 × 10³) = 6 × 10⁷

Divide: (8 × 10⁶) ÷ (4 × 10²) = 2 × 10⁴

Add/Subtract: first make the powers equal, then add M values.

M must satisfy 1 ≤ M < 10. If M = 12.5, rewrite: 12.5 × 10³ = 1.25 × 10⁴

Comparing Large and Small Numbers

Comparing in Scientific Notation

To compare, first compare the powers of 10:

  • Higher power = larger number (for positive numbers)
  • If powers equal, compare the M values

Example: Which is larger — 4.5 × 10⁸ or 9.1 × 10⁷?

10⁸ > 10⁷, so 4.5 × 10⁸ is larger.

Example: Which is smaller — 3.2 × 10⁻⁵ or 7.8 × 10⁻⁶?

10⁻⁶ < 10⁻⁵, so 7.8 × 10⁻⁶ is smaller.

Ratio of Two Quantities

Example: How many times is the mass of Earth greater than that of the Moon?

Mass of Earth: 5.98 × 10²⁴ kg

Mass of Moon: 7.36 × 10²² kg

Ratio = (5.98 × 10²⁴) ÷ (7.36 × 10²²)

= (5.98/7.36) × 10² ≈ 0.812 × 100 ≈ 81.2

Earth is about 81 times heavier than the Moon.

Scientific notation lets us compare quantities differing by billions — impossible to do easily with ordinary decimal notation.

Real-World Applications of Powers

Astronomical Distances

Distance from Earth to Sun:

≈ 149,600,000 km = 1.496 × 10⁸ km

Distance from Earth to Moon:

≈ 384,400 km = 3.844 × 10⁵ km

Diameter of the Sun:

≈ 1,391,000 km = 1.391 × 10⁶ km

Speed of light:

3 × 10⁸ m/s

Light from Sun takes: (1.496 × 10¹¹ m) ÷ (3 × 10⁸ m/s) ≈ 499 s ≈ 8.3 min

Microscopic Sizes

Diameter of a bacterium:

≈ 0.000001 m = 1 × 10⁻⁶ m = 1 micrometre (μm)

Diameter of an atom:

≈ 1 × 10⁻¹⁰ m (1 Angstrom)

Diameter of a human hair:

≈ 7 × 10⁻⁵ m

Thickness of paper:

≈ 1 × 10⁻⁴ m

Computer Science

1 GB = 2³⁰ ≈ 10⁹ bytes. Exponents power our digital world!

From atoms to galaxies — powers of 10 span 40 orders of magnitude in the physical world.

Chapter Summary – Exponents and Powers

All Laws of Exponents

aᵐ × aⁿ = aᵐ⁺ⁿ

aᵐ ÷ aⁿ = aᵐ⁻ⁿ

(aᵐ)ⁿ = aᵐⁿ

(ab)ⁿ = aⁿbⁿ

(a/b)ⁿ = aⁿ/bⁿ

a⁰ = 1 (a ≠ 0)

a⁻ⁿ = 1/aⁿ (a ≠ 0)

Standard Form (Scientific Notation)

M × 10ⁿ, where 1 ≤ M < 10

Positive n → large number (move decimal right)

Negative n → small number (move decimal left)

Key Reminders

  • Laws apply only to the same base (Laws 1–3) or same exponent (Law 4–5)
  • a⁻ⁿ ≠ −aⁿ: negative exponent means reciprocal, not negative number
  • (−a)ⁿ = aⁿ if n even; −aⁿ if n odd
