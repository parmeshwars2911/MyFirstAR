Chapter 12

Friction

The hidden force that affects every movement — friend and foe in the physical world

Grade 8 Science

What is Friction?

Friction is a force that opposes the relative motion (or tendency of motion) between two surfaces that are in contact with each other.
  • Friction always acts in the direction opposite to the direction of motion or attempted motion
  • It is a contact force — it only acts between surfaces touching each other
  • The unit of friction is the Newton (N) like all forces

Cause of Friction

Even surfaces that look smooth under a microscope are actually covered with tiny bumps and grooves called irregularities or asperities. When two surfaces come into contact:

  • Their asperities (microscopic peaks and valleys) interlock with each other
  • This interlocking resists sliding motion between the surfaces
  • The rougher the surfaces, the more interlocking → greater friction
  • There is also adhesion (molecular attraction) between surfaces at very close contact
Friction is caused by the interlocking of irregularities (asperities) on the surfaces of objects in contact.

Types of Friction

Friction is not a single type of force — it varies depending on whether the object is stationary, sliding, rolling, or moving through a fluid.

  • Static Friction: The friction that acts when an object is at rest and a force is trying to move it. It prevents the object from starting to move.
    Static friction adjusts itself up to a maximum value — if applied force exceeds the maximum static friction, the object begins to move.
    Example: A heavy box that does not move even when you push gently
  • Sliding (Kinetic) Friction: The friction that acts when an object is already sliding over another surface. It is slightly less than maximum static friction.
    Example: A book sliding across a table; car brakes causing tyres to slide
  • Rolling Friction: The friction that acts when a round object (like a wheel or ball) rolls over a surface. It is the least type of friction.
    Example: A ball rolling on the floor; wheels of a car rolling on road
  • Fluid Friction (Drag): The resistance experienced by an object moving through a liquid or gas (fluid). Also called drag or viscous drag.
    Example: A swimmer moving through water; a parachute slowing a skydiver
Order of friction (least to greatest): Rolling < Sliding < Static

Factors Affecting Friction

The magnitude of friction depends on specific factors related to the surfaces and the forces involved.

Factors that Affect Friction

  • Nature of surfaces: Rougher surfaces have more irregularities → more interlocking → greater friction. Smoother surfaces have less friction.
  • Normal force (weight of object): A heavier object presses down harder, causing deeper interlocking of asperities → greater friction. Doubling the weight doubles friction.

Factor that Does NOT Affect Friction

  • Area of contact: Surprisingly, friction does NOT depend on the area of contact between surfaces. A large block and a small block of the same weight experience the same friction.
Friction ∝ Normal Force (N)
Friction is independent of the area of contact.
Friction Force (f) = μ × N
where μ (mu) = coefficient of friction, N = Normal force

The coefficient of friction (μ) depends on the pair of surfaces in contact. It is higher for rough surfaces and lower for smooth or lubricated surfaces.

Advantages and Disadvantages of Friction

Advantages of Friction

  • Walking: Friction between shoe soles and ground allows us to walk without slipping
  • Vehicle brakes: Friction between brake pads and wheels slows/stops vehicles
  • Writing: Friction between pen/pencil and paper allows writing and drawing
  • Gripping objects: Friction lets us hold cups, tools, and other objects
  • Striking matches: Friction between matchstick and rough surface produces heat to ignite the match
  • Climbing trees/ropes: Friction allows grip

Disadvantages of Friction

  • Wear and tear: Friction gradually wears down surfaces — soles of shoes, tyres, machine parts all wear out
  • Heat generation: Friction converts kinetic energy to heat — wastes energy and can damage components
  • Energy loss: In machines, friction reduces efficiency; energy must be wasted to overcome it
  • Noise: Friction between moving parts causes unwanted noise and vibrations
  • Requires more force input in machines to overcome friction losses

Methods to Increase and Reduce Friction

Methods to Reduce Friction

  • Lubricants: Oil, grease, and graphite fill in surface irregularities, creating a smooth layer between moving parts. Used in engines, bicycle chains, door hinges.
  • Ball bearings: Replace sliding friction with rolling friction. Metal balls between moving parts reduce friction significantly. Used in wheels, fans, electric motors.
  • Streamlining: Giving vehicles (cars, aircraft, ships, fish) smooth, tapered shapes that reduce fluid friction (drag) as they move through air or water.
  • Polishing surfaces: Making surfaces smoother reduces interlocking of asperities
  • Using wheels and rollers instead of dragging objects

Methods to Increase Friction

  • Rough surfaces: Sandpaper, grooves, and textured materials increase friction where grip is needed
  • Treads on tyres: The pattern on tyre surfaces channels away water and provides grip on wet roads
  • Treads on shoe soles: Prevent slipping on smooth/wet surfaces
  • Spikes on sports shoes: Athletes wear spiked shoes for better grip on track/grass
  • Spreading sand/grit on icy roads in winter
  • Using brake pads with high friction coefficient

Chapter 12 Summary: Friction

  • Friction: Force that opposes relative motion between surfaces in contact; caused by interlocking of surface asperities (irregularities)
  • Static friction: Acts before motion starts; the object remains stationary until applied force exceeds it
  • Sliding (kinetic) friction: Acts while object slides; slightly less than static friction
  • Rolling friction: Acts when object rolls; the least of the three types
  • Fluid friction (drag): Resistance in liquids and gases; affected by object shape and fluid viscosity
  • Factors affecting friction: Surface roughness and normal force (NOT area of contact)
  • Advantages: Walking, braking, writing, gripping, striking matches
  • Disadvantages: Wear and tear, heat generation, energy loss, noise
  • Reducing friction: Lubricants, ball bearings, streamlining, polishing
  • Increasing friction: Rough surfaces, treads on tyres/shoes, sand on icy roads
