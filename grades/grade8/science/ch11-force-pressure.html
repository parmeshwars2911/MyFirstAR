Chapter 11

Force and Pressure

Understanding how pushes, pulls, and concentrated forces shape the physical world around us

Grade 8 Science

What is Force?

Force is a push or pull that can change the state of rest or motion, the speed, the direction, or the shape of an object. Force is a vector quantity — it has both magnitude and direction.

Effects of Force

  • Move a stationary object (push a trolley)
  • Stop a moving object (catching a ball)
  • Change the speed of a moving object (accelerate or decelerate)
  • Change the direction of a moving object (turning a car)
  • Change the shape or size of an object (squeezing clay)

Contact Forces

  • Friction: Opposes motion between surfaces in contact
  • Muscular force: Force exerted by muscles (lifting, pushing)
  • Normal force: Perpendicular support force from a surface

Non-Contact Forces

  • Gravitational force: Attraction between masses; pulls objects toward Earth
  • Magnetic force: Force exerted by magnets on magnetic materials or other magnets
  • Electrostatic force: Force between electrically charged objects

Resultant Force: Balanced and Unbalanced Forces

When more than one force acts on an object, the combined effect is called the resultant force (or net force).

Balanced Forces

When two or more forces acting on an object are equal in magnitude but opposite in direction, the net force = 0. The object does not change its state of motion.
  • A book lying on a table: gravity pulls it down, the table pushes it up equally
  • A tug-of-war where neither team moves: both sides pull equally
  • Result: No acceleration — object remains stationary or moves at constant speed

Unbalanced Forces

When the net force on an object is NOT zero, the object accelerates (speeds up), decelerates (slows down), or changes direction.
  • Kicking a ball: the kick force is unbalanced, causing the ball to move
  • Braking a bicycle: friction creates an unbalanced force, slowing the cycle
  • Result: Change in speed or direction (acceleration or deceleration)

The unit of force is the Newton (N), named after Sir Isaac Newton.

What is Pressure?

Pressure is defined as the force acting per unit area of a surface.
P = F / A    where P = Pressure, F = Force (in Newtons), A = Area (in m²)
  • Unit of pressure: Pascal (Pa) = 1 Newton per square metre (N/m²)
  • Same force over a smaller area = greater pressure
  • Same force over a larger area = lesser pressure

Increasing Pressure

  • Knife/blade edge: Tiny cutting area concentrates force, producing high pressure to cut easily
  • Nail/needle: Pointed tip = very small area = very high pressure
  • High heels: Concentrate body weight on tiny heel area → high pressure, can sink into soft ground

Decreasing Pressure

  • Snowshoes: Wide base spreads weight over large area → low pressure → don't sink in snow
  • Wide tyres on tractors: Distribute weight to avoid sinking in mud
  • Flat foundations of buildings: Spread the building's weight over a large area

Pressure in Fluids

Fluids (liquids and gases) exert pressure in all directions. The pressure in a fluid increases with depth.

Pressure in a liquid at depth h: P = ρgh where ρ (rho) = density of liquid, g = gravitational acceleration, h = depth

Key Facts about Fluid Pressure

  • Pressure at a point in a fluid depends on the depth below the surface, not the shape of the container
  • At the same depth, pressure is equal in all directions (Pascal's principle)
  • Deep-sea creatures withstand enormous water pressure due to their body design
  • Dams are built thicker at the base because water pressure is greatest there

Atmospheric Pressure

  • The atmosphere (air column above Earth) exerts pressure on all objects
  • 1 atmosphere (atm) = 101,325 Pa ≈ 1.013 × 10⁵ Pa
  • Barometer: Instrument used to measure atmospheric pressure
  • At higher altitudes (mountains), the air column above is shorter → lower atmospheric pressure → difficulty breathing (less oxygen)
  • At sea level, atmospheric pressure is maximum

Applications of Pressure

  • Dams: Built with thicker walls at the base because water pressure increases with depth
  • Syringe/Injections: The plunger reduces pressure inside the barrel, causing liquid to be drawn in or pushed out
  • Hydraulic lift (Pascal's Law): Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted equally throughout. A small force on a small piston creates a large force on a larger piston. Used in car lifts, hydraulic brakes, and cranes.
  • Suction cups: Remove air to create low pressure, and atmospheric pressure holds them in place
  • Blood pressure: The pressure exerted by blood against the walls of blood vessels; measured with a sphygmomanometer (120/80 mmHg is normal)
  • Scuba diving gear: Regulates air pressure to match water pressure at depth
Pascal's Law: Pressure applied to an enclosed fluid is transmitted undiminished to every part of the fluid and the walls of the container.

Chapter 11 Summary: Force and Pressure

  • Force: Push or pull; can change shape, speed, or direction of motion
  • Contact forces: Friction, muscular force; act through direct contact
  • Non-contact forces: Gravity, magnetic force, electrostatic force; act at a distance
  • Balanced forces: Net force = 0; no change in motion
  • Unbalanced forces: Net force ≠ 0; causes acceleration or deceleration
  • Pressure: Force per unit area; same force on smaller area = more pressure
  • Atmospheric pressure: 1 atm = 101,325 Pa; measured by barometer; decreases with altitude
  • Pascal's Law: Pressure in enclosed fluid is transmitted equally in all directions — basis of hydraulic systems
