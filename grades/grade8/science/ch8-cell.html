Cell – Structure and Functions

Chapter 8 · Grade 8 Science

The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of all living organisms. Just as bricks make a building, cells make up every living thing — from a tiny bacterium to a blue whale, and from a blade of grass to a towering banyan tree.

Topics covered: Discovery of cells · Cell theory · Unicellular vs multicellular · Plant vs animal cells · Cell organelles · Functions of organelles

Discovery of Cell and Cell Theory

Historical Timeline

  • 1665 – Robert Hooke: Discovered the cell while examining thin slices of cork under a self-made microscope. He saw tiny box-like compartments and called them "cells" (from Latin "cellula" = small room). He was actually observing dead plant cell walls.
  • 1674 – Anton van Leeuwenhoek: First to observe living cells (bacteria and protozoa) in pond water using an improved microscope. Called them "animalcules."
  • 1831 – Robert Brown: Discovered the nucleus inside cells.
  • 1839 – Matthias Schleiden: Concluded that all plants are made of cells.
  • 1839 – Theodor Schwann: Concluded that all animals are also made of cells.
  • 1855 – Rudolf Virchow: Proposed that all cells arise from pre-existing cells ("Omnis cellula e cellula").

Cell Theory (Schleiden, Schwann, Virchow)

The three principles of cell theory:

  • All living organisms are composed of one or more cells
  • The cell is the basic structural and functional unit of life
  • All cells arise from pre-existing cells by cell division

Cell Size and Shape

  • Most cells are microscopic (1–100 micrometres)
  • Largest cell: Ostrich egg yolk (~170 mm diameter)
  • Longest cell: Nerve cell (up to 1 metre in length)
  • Cells can be spherical, cylindrical, disc-shaped, spindle-shaped, irregular
  • Shape usually relates to function

Unicellular vs Multicellular Organisms

Unicellular Organisms

Composed of a single cell that performs ALL life functions.

  • The one cell obtains food, excretes waste, responds to stimuli, reproduces, and maintains itself
  • Examples:
    • Amoeba – changes shape, moves by pseudopodia
    • Paramecium – moves using cilia
    • Euglena – can photosynthesise and move
    • Chlamydomonas – unicellular alga
    • Bacteria – prokaryotic unicellular organisms
  • Most are microscopic and found in water and soil

Multicellular Organisms

Composed of many cells, each specialised for a specific function.

  • Cells are organised into tissues → organs → organ systems
  • Division of labour: different cells do different jobs
  • Examples: Humans, elephants, tigers, trees, ferns, fungi
  • Specialised cells in humans:
    • Red blood cells – carry oxygen
    • Nerve cells – carry messages
    • Muscle cells – enable movement
    • Guard cells (plants) – control gas exchange

A human body contains approximately 37 trillion cells of about 200 different types!

Plant Cell vs Animal Cell

Both plant and animal cells share many structures, but there are important differences.

Plant Cell

  • Has a rigid cell wall made of cellulose (outside the cell membrane) — provides shape and support
  • Has chloroplasts — organelles containing chlorophyll for photosynthesis
  • Has a large, central vacuole (up to 90% of cell volume) — stores water, sap, and maintains cell pressure (turgor)
  • May have plastids (chloroplasts, chromoplasts, leucoplasts)
  • Generally rectangular/box-shaped due to rigid cell wall
  • Centrioles absent in most plant cells

Animal Cell

  • No cell wall — only a flexible cell membrane; cells can change shape
  • No chloroplasts — cannot photosynthesise; must eat food
  • Vacuoles small and temporary (or absent)
  • Has centrioles — involved in cell division
  • Generally rounded or irregular in shape
  • May have lysosomes (digestive organelles)

Common to both: nucleus, cell membrane, cytoplasm, mitochondria, ribosomes, endoplasmic reticulum, Golgi apparatus.

Cell Organelles

Organelles are specialised structures within a cell that perform specific functions — like organs in the body.

  • Cell membrane (Plasma membrane): Thin, flexible layer surrounding all cells. Selectively permeable — controls what enters and exits the cell. Made of lipids and proteins.
  • Cell wall (in plants only): Rigid outer layer made of cellulose. Provides structural support and protection. Fully permeable.
  • Nucleus: Control centre of the cell. Contains DNA (genetic information). Surrounded by a nuclear membrane with pores. Contains the nucleolus (produces ribosomes). Directs cell activities and reproduction.
  • Cytoplasm: Jelly-like fluid (cytosol) filling the cell between the nucleus and cell membrane. Suspends all organelles. Site of many chemical reactions.
  • Mitochondria: "Powerhouse of the cell." Produces ATP (energy) through cellular respiration. Has its own DNA. Double-membraned with inner folds called cristae.
  • Chloroplasts (plants only): Site of photosynthesis. Contains chlorophyll (green pigment) that captures light energy. Has its own DNA. Double-membraned with internal thylakoid membranes (grana).
  • Vacuole: Storage organelle. Large central vacuole in plants (stores water, sap, waste). Small, temporary vacuoles in animal cells.
  • Endoplasmic Reticulum (ER): Network of membranes. Rough ER (with ribosomes) makes proteins; Smooth ER makes lipids.
  • Ribosome: Site of protein synthesis. Found on rough ER and floating freely in cytoplasm.
  • Golgi apparatus: Packages and ships proteins/lipids made in the ER. Called the "post office" of the cell.

Functions of Key Organelles

Nucleus

  • Contains chromosomes (DNA) — blueprints for all proteins and characteristics
  • Controls cell growth, metabolism, and reproduction
  • Directs protein synthesis via RNA
  • During cell division, chromosomes condense and are visible
  • Cells without a nucleus (red blood cells) cannot reproduce
  • Prokaryotes (bacteria) have no membrane-bound nucleus — DNA floats freely in cytoplasm

Mitochondria

  • Carries out aerobic cellular respiration:
Glucose + O2 → CO2 + H2O + ATP (energy)
  • Cells with high energy needs (muscle cells, liver cells) have more mitochondria

Chloroplast

  • Site of photosynthesis — only in plant cells and algae
  • Captures light energy using chlorophyll:
CO2 + H2O + light → Glucose + O2
  • Gives plants their green colour
  • Leucoplasts store starch; chromoplasts give flower/fruit colours

Vacuole

  • In plant cells: maintains turgor pressure — keeps cells firm; stores water, minerals, pigments, waste
  • In Amoeba: food vacuoles digest food; contractile vacuoles pump out excess water
  • In animal cells: small, transient, used for digestion and storage

Cell membrane controls movement of substances by osmosis (water) and diffusion (dissolved substances).

Chapter Summary

Key Concepts

  • Robert Hooke (1665) discovered cells; Anton van Leeuwenhoek first saw living cells
  • Cell theory: all life is made of cells; cells arise from cells (Schleiden, Schwann, Virchow)
  • Unicellular (one cell does everything) vs multicellular (specialised cells)
  • Plant cells have: cell wall, chloroplasts, large vacuole (absent in animal cells)
  • Animal cells have: centrioles; no cell wall, no chloroplasts
  • Nucleus = control centre; Mitochondria = powerhouse; Chloroplast = food factory (plants)

Key Terms

  • Cell: Basic structural and functional unit of life
  • Organelle: Specialised structure within a cell
  • Nucleus: Membrane-bound DNA-containing organelle; cell's control centre
  • Mitochondria: ATP-producing "powerhouse" organelle
  • Chloroplast: Photosynthesis organelle in plant cells
  • Cell wall: Rigid cellulose layer in plant cells
  • Vacuole: Storage organelle; large in plants
  • Cytoplasm: Fluid filling the cell interior

Understanding cells is the foundation of all biology — from understanding how medicines work, to how diseases spread, to how we might one day grow replacement organs in the lab.
