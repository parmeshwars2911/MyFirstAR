Light

Exploring reflection, refraction, dispersion, and the remarkable organ that allows us to see — the human eye

Reflection of Light

Reflection is the bouncing back of light when it strikes a smooth, shiny surface such as a mirror.

Laws of Reflection

  • Law 1: The angle of incidence (i) is always equal to the angle of reflection (r).
    ∠i = ∠r
  • Law 2: The incident ray, the reflected ray, and the normal (perpendicular to the surface at the point of incidence) all lie in the same plane.

Key Terms

  • Incident ray: The ray of light that falls on the reflecting surface
  • Reflected ray: The ray of light that bounces back from the surface
  • Normal: An imaginary perpendicular line drawn at the point where the incident ray hits the mirror
  • Angle of incidence (i): Angle between the incident ray and the normal
  • Angle of reflection (r): Angle between the reflected ray and the normal
∠i = ∠r (First Law of Reflection)

These laws apply to all reflecting surfaces — flat mirrors, curved mirrors, and even rough surfaces (though the direction of reflection differs).

Types of Reflection

Regular (Specular) Reflection

Occurs when light reflects from a smooth, polished surface such as a mirror or still water. All reflected rays are parallel, producing a clear, sharp image.
  • All incident rays hitting a smooth surface reflect at the same angle relative to the normal
  • Reflected rays are all parallel to each other → forms a clear, undistorted image
  • Examples: Flat mirror, calm lake surface, polished metal, glass
  • Used in optical instruments, mirrors, telescopes, periscopes

Diffuse (Irregular) Reflection

Occurs when light reflects from a rough or uneven surface. Reflected rays scatter in many different directions, so no clear image is formed.
  • Each part of the rough surface has its own orientation of normal, so rays reflect in different directions
  • No image is formed, but light is scattered throughout the room
  • Examples: Walls, paper, wood, cloth, human skin
  • This is why we can see non-luminous objects — diffuse reflection from their surfaces reaches our eyes from many directions
  • Allows even illumination of rooms (light scatters everywhere)

Both types of reflection obey the same laws of reflection — the difference is in the orientation of the surface at the microscopic level.

Spherical Mirrors

Spherical mirrors are curved mirrors whose reflecting surface is part of a sphere. There are two types:

Concave Mirror

The reflecting surface curves inward (like the inside of a spoon). It is a converging mirror — parallel rays of light reflect and meet at a focal point in front of the mirror.
  • Centre of curvature (C): Centre of the sphere of which the mirror forms a part
  • Principal focus (F): Point where parallel rays converge after reflection; lies between the mirror and C
  • Focal length (f): Distance from mirror to focus; f = R/2 (R = radius of curvature)
  • Can form real, inverted images (when object is beyond F) or virtual, magnified images (when object is between F and mirror)

Uses of Concave Mirror

  • Torches and vehicle headlights (parallel beam of light)
  • Shaving and makeup mirrors (magnified, upright image)
  • Solar furnaces (concentrates sunlight at focus)
  • Dentist's mirror (to see magnified view of teeth)

Convex Mirror

The reflecting surface curves outward (like the outside of a spoon). It is a diverging mirror — parallel rays of light reflect and appear to diverge from a focal point behind the mirror.
  • Always forms a virtual, erect, and diminished (smaller) image regardless of object position
  • Provides a wider field of view than a flat mirror of the same size
  • The focal point is virtual (behind the mirror)

Uses of Convex Mirror

  • Rear-view mirrors in vehicles (wider field of view, see more of the road behind)
  • Security mirrors in shops and at road corners (wide-angle view)
  • On sharp road bends to reveal oncoming traffic
  • In ATMs (to see who is standing behind you)

Refraction of Light

Refraction is the bending of light when it passes from one transparent medium to another, due to a change in the speed of light in different media.
  • Light travels at different speeds in different media: fastest in vacuum (3 × 10⁸ m/s), slower in glass, water, etc.
  • When light moves from a less dense medium to a denser medium (e.g., air to glass), it slows down and bends toward the normal
  • When light moves from a denser to a less dense medium (e.g., glass to air), it speeds up and bends away from the normal
  • If light hits the surface at 90° (along the normal), it passes straight through without bending
Refractive Index (n) = Speed of light in vacuum / Speed of light in medium
n = c / v

Everyday Examples of Refraction

  • Coin in water appears raised: Light from the coin bends away from normal as it exits water, making the coin appear higher than it is
  • Pencil in water appears bent: The part of the pencil in water appears displaced and bent at the water surface
  • Swimming pool appears shallower than it actually is
  • Twinkling of stars: Light from stars is refracted by different layers of the atmosphere

Dispersion of Light and the Human Eye

Dispersion of White Light

When white light passes through a glass prism, it splits into its component colours — a band of colours called a spectrum. This is called dispersion.
  • The colours of the spectrum: VIBGYOR — Violet, Indigo, Blue, Green, Yellow, Orange, Red
  • Violet bends the most (highest refractive index); Red bends the least
  • Rainbow: Formed when sunlight is dispersed by tiny water droplets in the atmosphere after rain; sunlight enters the droplet, refracts, internally reflects, and disperses
  • Rainbows are always seen with the sun behind the observer

The Human Eye

  • Cornea: Transparent front surface; does most of the focusing of light
  • Iris: Coloured part; controls the size of the pupil
  • Pupil: Opening that controls how much light enters the eye
  • Lens: Flexible, adjusts curvature to focus near and far objects (accommodation)
  • Retina: Light-sensitive inner layer; contains rods (for dim light/black-and-white) and cones (for colour and bright light)
  • Optic nerve: Carries electrical signals from retina to the brain

Eye Defects

  • Myopia (short-sightedness): Can see near objects clearly; far objects blurry; corrected by concave (diverging) lens
  • Hypermetropia (long-sightedness): Can see far objects clearly; near objects blurry; corrected by convex (converging) lens
  • Cataract: Clouding of the eye's lens; causes blurry vision; treated by surgical replacement of the lens

Chapter 16 Summary: Light

  • Reflection: Bouncing of light off a surface; Laws: ∠i = ∠r; incident ray, reflected ray, and normal are coplanar
  • Regular reflection: From smooth surfaces (mirrors); gives clear images; all reflected rays parallel
  • Diffuse reflection: From rough surfaces (walls, paper); scatters light; lets us see non-luminous objects
  • Concave mirror: Inward-curving; converging; used in torches, shaving mirrors, solar furnaces, dentist mirrors
  • Convex mirror: Outward-curving; diverging; always gives virtual, erect, smaller image; used in rear-view and security mirrors
  • Refraction: Bending of light passing between media due to change in speed; bends toward normal entering denser medium; bends away entering less dense medium
  • Refractive index (n): n = speed in vacuum / speed in medium; higher n = more bending
  • Examples of refraction: Coin in water appears raised, pencil in water appears bent, pool looks shallower
  • Dispersion: White light splits into VIBGYOR through a prism; violet bends most, red bends least; rainbow = natural dispersion
  • Eye defects: Myopia → concave lens; Hypermetropia → convex lens; Cataract → lens replacement surgery
