Conservation of Plants and Animals

Chapter 7 · Grade 8 Science

Earth's biodiversity — the variety of all living organisms — is under serious threat. Forests are being cut, species are disappearing, and ecosystems are collapsing. Conservation is our collective responsibility.

Topics covered: Deforestation · Forest conservation · Biosphere reserves · Flora and fauna · Endangered species · Migration and recycling

Deforestation: Causes and Consequences

Deforestation means clearing forests on a large scale. It is one of the most serious environmental problems today.

Causes of Deforestation

  • Agricultural expansion: Clearing forests to create farmland to feed growing populations
  • Urbanisation: Forests cleared for housing, roads, factories, and infrastructure
  • Industrialisation: Mining, power plants, and industrial zones replace forests
  • Timber and wood: Cutting trees for wood (furniture, paper, fuel)
  • Forest fires: Accidental or deliberate burning of forests
  • Overgrazing: Livestock eat and damage young forest growth

Consequences of Deforestation

  • Floods: Tree roots hold soil and absorb rainwater; without them, water rushes off rapidly
  • Droughts: Fewer trees means less water vapour released (transpiration), reducing rainfall
  • Desertification: Fertile land turns into desert as soil erodes and dries out
  • Global warming: Fewer trees absorb less CO2; felled trees release stored carbon
  • Loss of biodiversity: Wild animals lose habitat; many species face extinction
  • Soil erosion: Without tree roots, topsoil is washed away by rain and wind

India loses about 1.5 million hectares of forest cover each year to various human activities.

Conservation of Forests

Forest conservation involves protecting existing forests and restoring those that have been destroyed.

Reforestation and Afforestation

  • Afforestation: Planting new trees in areas that previously had no forest cover
  • Reforestation: Replanting trees in areas where forests have been cut or destroyed
  • Van Mahotsav: Annual tree-planting festival in India (July) — millions of trees planted
  • Social forestry: Growing trees on community land for local use
  • Agroforestry: Growing trees alongside crops — protects soil and provides additional income

Protected Areas

  • National Parks: Large protected areas where wildlife and ecosystems are preserved. No human activity (including grazing, timber collection) is allowed. Examples: Jim Corbett, Kanha, Ranthambore, Bandipur.
  • Wildlife Sanctuaries: Areas protected for conservation of specific wildlife. Limited human activities may be allowed. Examples: Chilika (birds), Kaziranga (rhinos), Bharatpur (birds).
  • Reserved Forests: Forest areas managed by the government; limited human access.
  • Protected Forests: Some activities permitted under regulation.

Project Tiger (1973) and Project Elephant are major Indian conservation programmes. India now has more than 50 tiger reserves.

Biosphere Reserves

A biosphere reserve is a large area of natural land designated to conserve the biodiversity of an entire ecosystem — including plants, animals, and human communities.

Features of Biosphere Reserves

  • Protect entire ecosystems (not just individual species)
  • Three zones:
    • Core zone: Strictly protected; no human activity allowed
    • Buffer zone: Limited research and tourism permitted
    • Transition zone: Human settlements and sustainable activities allowed
  • Designated by UNESCO under the Man and Biosphere (MAB) Programme
  • India has 18 biosphere reserves

Important Biosphere Reserves in India

  • Pachmarhi Biosphere Reserve (Madhya Pradesh): Includes Satpura National Park and Bori and Pachmarhi sanctuaries. Rich in sal and teak forests, leopards, wild boars.
  • Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve (South India): First in India (1986). Spans Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka. Home to elephants, tigers, leopards, Nilgiri tahr.
  • Sundarbans (West Bengal): World's largest mangrove forest; Royal Bengal Tiger habitat.
  • Gulf of Mannar: First marine biosphere reserve in South/Southeast Asia.

Flora, Fauna, Endemic and Endangered Species

Key Definitions

  • Flora: All the plant life found in a particular region or time period. (Latin: Flora = goddess of flowers)
  • Fauna: All the animal life found in a particular region. Together, flora and fauna represent the biodiversity of an area.
  • Endemic species: Species found exclusively in a particular geographic area and nowhere else on Earth. Example: Asiatic lion (only in Gir forest, Gujarat), Lion-tailed macaque (Western Ghats).
  • Biodiversity: The variety of all living species in a given ecosystem, region, or on Earth.

Endangered Species and Red Data Book

  • Endangered species: Species whose population has declined so drastically that they face the risk of extinction. Example: Bengal tiger, Snow leopard, One-horned rhinoceros, Olive ridley turtle.
  • Vulnerable species: At high risk of becoming endangered.
  • Extinct species: No longer exist anywhere on Earth. Example: Dodo, Passenger pigeon, Dinosaurs.
  • Red Data Book: Published by IUCN (International Union for Conservation of Nature); lists all threatened (endangered, vulnerable, critically endangered) species worldwide. Maintained since 1964.

Every species lost is an irreversible loss — extinction is forever. Currently, over 40,000 species are threatened with extinction.

Migration and Recycling of Paper

Migration

Migration is the seasonal movement of animals from one place to another in search of food, suitable climate, or breeding grounds.

  • Migratory birds travel thousands of kilometres across continents each year
  • Example: Siberian cranes migrate to Bharatpur (Rajasthan) in winter
  • Example: Flamingos come to Rann of Kutch (Gujarat) for breeding
  • Example: Amur falcons migrate from Siberia/China to South Africa via India (Nagaland)
  • Fish, whales, caribou, monarch butterflies also migrate
  • Deforestation and habitat loss disrupt migration routes, threatening migratory species

Migratory species cross international borders — their conservation requires global cooperation.

Recycling of Paper

Paper is made from wood pulp — cutting millions of trees. Recycling paper saves forests and energy.

  • 17 trees are saved by recycling 1 tonne of paper
  • Recycling 1 tonne of paper saves 26,500 litres of water
  • Reduces energy consumption by 60–70% compared to making paper from fresh pulp
  • How to help:
    • Use both sides of paper
    • Save old newspapers and magazines for recycling
    • Use recycled paper products
    • Prefer digital communication over print

Chapter Summary

Key Concepts

  • Deforestation causes floods, droughts, soil erosion, global warming, and biodiversity loss
  • Conservation: afforestation, reforestation, national parks, wildlife sanctuaries, biosphere reserves
  • Pachmarhi and Nilgiri are important biosphere reserves in India
  • Endemic species found only in specific regions; endangered species risk extinction
  • Red Data Book (IUCN) lists threatened species worldwide
  • Recycling paper saves trees; migration needs habitat protection

Key Terms

  • Deforestation: Large-scale clearing of forests
  • Afforestation: Planting trees in new areas
  • Biosphere reserve: Area conserving entire ecosystem biodiversity
  • Endemic species: Found only in a specific region
  • Endangered species: At risk of extinction
  • Red Data Book: IUCN's record of threatened species
  • Migration: Seasonal movement of animals to new habitats
  • Flora/Fauna: Plants/animals of a region

Conservation is not just a scientific issue — it is a moral obligation. Every species we protect, every tree we plant, and every page we recycle contributes to a healthier planet.
