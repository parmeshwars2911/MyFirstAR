Chapter 13

Sound

From vibrating objects to our ears — exploring how sound is produced, travels, and is perceived

Grade 8 Science

Production and Propagation of Sound

Sound is produced by the vibration of objects. Any object that vibrates produces sound. Sound needs a medium (solid, liquid, or gas) to travel — it cannot travel in vacuum.

How Sound is Produced

  • Vibrating tuning fork: Strike a tuning fork and touch it to water — you see ripples showing the vibration. Place it near your ear and you hear the sound.
  • Vocal cords: When we speak, air from the lungs causes our vocal cords (in the larynx) to vibrate, producing sound
  • Musical instruments: Stretched strings (guitar, sitar) vibrate when plucked; membranes (drum, tabla) vibrate when struck; air columns in flutes vibrate when blown

Propagation of Sound

  • Sound travels as a wave by causing particles in the medium to vibrate
  • Sound travels fastest in solids, then in liquids, and slowest in gases
  • In vacuum, there are no particles to vibrate — so sound cannot travel (proven by the bell jar experiment: a ringing bell in a vacuum jar cannot be heard)

Nature of Sound Waves

Sound travels as longitudinal waves — the particles of the medium vibrate in the same direction as the direction of wave propagation (back and forth, not up and down).

Compressions and Rarefactions

  • Compression: A region where particles are pushed together — the air pressure is higher than normal. Compressions are the "crests" of a longitudinal wave.
  • Rarefaction: A region where particles are spread apart — the air pressure is lower than normal. Rarefactions are the "troughs" of a longitudinal wave.
  • Sound wave = alternating compressions and rarefactions moving through the medium

Wavelength

  • The wavelength (λ) of a sound wave is the distance between two consecutive compressions (or two consecutive rarefactions)
  • It is measured in metres (m)
  • Shorter wavelength = higher frequency = higher pitch sound
Unlike transverse waves (e.g., light, water surface waves), sound waves are longitudinal — meaning particle displacement is parallel to the direction of wave travel.

Properties of Sound Waves

Frequency (f)

  • Number of vibrations (cycles) per second
  • Unit: Hertz (Hz)
  • Higher frequency = higher pitch (shriller sound)
  • Lower frequency = lower pitch (deeper sound)

Amplitude (A)

  • Maximum displacement of a particle from its rest position
  • Determines the loudness of sound
  • Greater amplitude = louder sound
  • Loudness is measured in decibels (dB)

Speed of Sound

  • Speed in air at 20°C: 343 m/s (approximately 340 m/s)
  • Speed in water: ~1500 m/s (faster than in air)
  • Speed in steel: ~5000 m/s (fastest)
  • Sound travels faster in denser/more elastic media
  • Speed increases with temperature
Speed = Frequency × Wavelength
v = f × λ

Speed (v) in m/s, Frequency (f) in Hz, Wavelength (λ) in metres

Audible Range, Ultrasound, and Infrasound

The human ear can detect sounds in the frequency range of 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz. This is called the audible range.

Infrasound (Below 20 Hz)

  • Frequency below the human hearing range
  • Cannot be heard by humans
  • Used by elephants and whales to communicate over very long distances
  • Produced by earthquakes, volcanoes, and some industrial machinery

Ultrasound (Above 20,000 Hz)

  • Frequency above the human hearing range
  • Used by bats for echolocation — emit ultrasound pulses and listen to echoes to detect obstacles and prey in darkness
  • Used by dolphins for navigation and communication
  • Medical ultrasound scans (sonography) use ultrasound to image internal organs and developing foetuses
  • Used in ultrasonic cleaning of delicate items

SONAR

SONAR stands for Sound Navigation And Ranging. It uses ultrasound waves to detect objects underwater.
  • A transmitter sends ultrasound pulses into water
  • The pulses reflect off underwater objects (submarines, sea floor, fish) and return as echoes
  • Time taken for echo to return is used to calculate the distance
Distance = (Speed of sound × Time) / 2
d = v × t / 2
  • SONAR is used in submarines, ships, and to map the ocean floor
  • Bats and dolphins use biological SONAR (echolocation)

Noise Pollution

Noise pollution is unwanted or harmful sound in the environment that exceeds comfortable or safe levels. The threshold of pain is about 80 dB; prolonged exposure above this causes hearing damage.

Causes of Noise Pollution

  • Vehicles: Car horns, engine noise, traffic congestion
  • Industries: Heavy machinery, factories, construction sites
  • Loudspeakers: Music systems, public address systems, firecrackers during festivals
  • Construction: Drilling, hammering, bulldozers
  • Airports and railway stations
  • Domestic sources: TV, music systems, pressure cookers

Effects of Noise Pollution

  • Hearing loss (deafness): Prolonged exposure to loud sounds damages the delicate hair cells in the inner ear
  • Stress and anxiety: Constant noise keeps the body in a state of alertness/stress
  • Sleep disturbance: Noise prevents restful sleep, leading to fatigue
  • Hypertension: Chronic noise exposure raises blood pressure
  • Reduced concentration and poor academic/work performance

Prevention

  • Noise barriers (walls, sound-absorbing panels) along highways
  • Planting trees (act as natural sound absorbers)
  • Regulating vehicle and industrial noise levels
  • Using ear protection in noisy workplaces

Chapter 13 Summary: Sound

  • Sound production: Produced by vibration; requires a medium to travel; cannot travel in vacuum
  • Sound wave type: Longitudinal wave; travels as alternating compressions (high pressure) and rarefactions (low pressure)
  • Frequency: Vibrations per second (Hz); determines pitch — higher frequency = higher pitch
  • Amplitude: Maximum displacement; determines loudness — greater amplitude = louder sound
  • Speed of sound: 343 m/s in air (20°C); faster in liquids and solids
  • Audible range: 20 Hz to 20,000 Hz for humans
  • Infrasound: Below 20 Hz; used by elephants and whales
  • Ultrasound: Above 20,000 Hz; used by bats (echolocation), dolphins, SONAR, medical scans
  • Noise pollution: Causes hearing loss, stress, sleep disturbance, hypertension; prevented by barriers, trees, regulations
