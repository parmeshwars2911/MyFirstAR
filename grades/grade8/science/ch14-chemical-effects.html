Chapter 14

Chemical Effects of Electric Current

How electricity can decompose compounds, deposit metals, and drive powerful chemical reactions

Grade 8 Science

Conductors, Insulators, and Electrolytes

Conductors

Materials that allow electric current to pass through them easily.
  • Have free electrons that can move under an electric field
  • Examples: Metals — copper, silver, gold, aluminium, iron
  • Graphite (carbon) is a non-metal that conducts electricity
  • Used in wires, cables, and electrical components

Insulators

Materials that do not allow electric current to pass through them.
  • No free electrons available for current flow
  • Examples: Rubber, plastic, wood, glass, dry air
  • Used to cover electrical wires (insulation) for safety

Electrolytes

Solutions that conduct electricity are called electrolytes. They conduct electricity because they contain free ions (charged particles).
  • Salt water (NaCl solution): Na⁺ and Cl⁻ ions conduct current
  • Dilute hydrochloric acid (HCl): H⁺ and Cl⁻ ions
  • Dilute sulphuric acid (H₂SO₄): H⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions
  • Copper sulphate solution: Cu²⁺ and SO₄²⁻ ions

Non-Electrolytes

  • Distilled water: Pure water has no ions → does not conduct
  • Sugar solution: Sugar molecules do not ionize → does not conduct
  • Alcohol, oils — non-conducting

Electrolysis

Electrolysis is the process of using electric current to cause a chemical decomposition (breakdown) of an electrolyte. The electrolyte is broken down into its constituent elements at the electrodes.

Setup for Electrolysis

  • Electrolyte: The ionic solution being decomposed (e.g., water with a little sulphuric acid)
  • Electrodes: Two conducting rods dipped into the electrolyte
    • Anode (+): Connected to the positive terminal of the battery; positive electrode
    • Cathode (−): Connected to the negative terminal of the battery; negative electrode
  • Positive ions (cations) move toward the cathode (−); negative ions (anions) move toward the anode (+)

Example: Electrolysis of Water

  • Pure water is a poor conductor; add a little dilute H₂SO₄ or NaOH to improve conductivity
  • At cathode (−): H⁺ ions gain electrons → Hydrogen gas (H₂) is released
  • At anode (+): OH⁻ ions lose electrons → Oxygen gas (O₂) is released
  • Volume of hydrogen produced is twice the volume of oxygen (H₂O has 2 H : 1 O)
2H₂O → 2H₂ + O₂ (electrolysis)

Electroplating

Electroplating is the process of depositing a thin, uniform layer of a metal on another object (the object to be plated) using electrolysis.

Process of Electroplating

  • Object to be plated (cathode −): The object receiving the metal coating (e.g., an iron spoon to be silver-plated)
  • Plating metal (anode +): A piece of the metal to be deposited (e.g., a silver bar)
  • Electrolyte: A solution containing ions of the plating metal (e.g., silver nitrate solution for silver plating)
  • Metal ions from the anode dissolve into solution; metal ions from solution deposit on the cathode

Uses of Electroplating

  • Gold plating on jewelry: Cheaper base metal coated with gold for appearance
  • Chromium plating on car parts: Shiny, rust-resistant coating on bumpers, taps, bicycles
  • Tin-plating of iron cans: Tin does not react with food; protects iron cans used for food preservation
  • Silver plating: On cutlery, religious items, trophies
  • Zinc-plating (galvanisation): Protects iron from rusting

Why Electroplate?

  • Saves cost (expensive metal used only as thin layer)
  • Improves appearance and shine
  • Provides corrosion and rust protection
  • Increases wear resistance
  • Enables specific surface properties on cheap base metals

Chemical Effects of Electric Current

When electric current passes through a conducting solution, various chemical changes occur. These are the chemical effects of electric current.

  • Decomposition of compounds: Compounds break down into simpler substances (e.g., water into H₂ and O₂)
  • Evolution of gases: Gases are produced at the electrodes (H₂ at cathode, O₂ at anode during electrolysis of water)
  • Deposition of metals: Metal ions from solution deposit as solid metal on cathode (basis of electroplating)
  • Colour changes in solution: The solution may change colour as ions are consumed or produced

Testing Solutions for Conductivity

  • A simple tester circuit: battery, LED or bulb, two electrodes, and the test solution
  • If the LED glows brightly → good conductor (strong electrolyte: salt water, dilute acids)
  • If the LED glows dimly → weak conductor (weak electrolyte: lemon juice, tap water)
  • If the LED does not glow → non-conductor (distilled water, sugar solution, oil)
Even tap water conducts electricity slightly because it contains dissolved minerals and salts. This is why we must never touch electrical appliances with wet hands.

Industrial Applications of Electrolysis

Purification of Metals (Copper Refining)

Electrolytic refining is used to obtain very pure copper from impure copper.
  • Anode: Impure copper
  • Cathode: Pure copper (thin sheet)
  • Electrolyte: Copper sulphate solution
  • Impure copper dissolves from anode; pure copper deposits on cathode
  • Impurities (gold, silver, platinum) collect as "anode mud" below the anode
  • Used for aluminium, nickel, and zinc refining too

Electroforming

  • Producing exact metal replicas of objects using electrolysis
  • Used in making coins and medals — a wax or plastic mold is electroplated with metal to create a precise copy
  • Printing plates for newspapers and books are made this way (electrotyping)
  • Making complex-shaped metal parts that are difficult to machine

Electrotyping

  • Making printing plates from typeset material
  • A mold of the type is made, then electroplated with metal to form the printing plate
  • Produces very detailed, durable printing surfaces

Chapter 14 Summary: Chemical Effects of Electric Current

  • Conductors: Allow current flow (metals, graphite); Insulators: do not allow current flow (rubber, plastic)
  • Electrolytes: Solutions that conduct electricity via ions (salt water, dilute HCl, dilute H₂SO₄, CuSO₄ solution)
  • Non-electrolytes: Do not conduct electricity (distilled water, sugar solution)
  • Electrolysis: Chemical decomposition using electric current; anode (+) and cathode (−) dipped in electrolyte
  • Electrolysis of water: H₂ gas at cathode, O₂ gas at anode; volume ratio H₂:O₂ = 2:1
  • Electroplating: Depositing thin metal layer on an object (cathode) using electrolysis; used for gold, silver, chromium, tin plating
  • Chemical effects of current: Decomposition, gas evolution, metal deposition, colour changes
  • Copper refining: Impure Cu anode → dissolves; pure Cu deposits on cathode from CuSO₄ solution
  • Electroforming/Electrotyping: Making exact metal replicas for coins, medals, printing plates
