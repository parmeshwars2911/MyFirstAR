Combustion and Flame

Chapter 6 · Grade 8 Science

Every time we light a candle, cook food, or drive a car, combustion is at work. Combustion is a chemical reaction that releases heat and light energy. Understanding it helps us use fire safely and efficiently.

Topics covered: What is combustion · Types of combustion · Structure of flame · Fuels and calorific value · Harmful effects of burning

Combustion: Definition and Conditions

Combustion is a chemical process in which a substance (fuel) reacts with oxygen to produce heat and light.

Fuel + Oxygen → Carbon dioxide + Water + Heat + Light

Three essential conditions must all be present for combustion to occur (the Fire Triangle):

The Three Requirements

  • Fuel (combustible material): The substance that burns — wood, LPG, petrol, coal, paper, wax
  • Air / Oxygen (supporter of combustion): Oxygen in air is necessary for combustion. Without air, a flame goes out (principle used in fire extinguishers).
  • Ignition temperature: The minimum temperature at which a substance starts burning. Different fuels have different ignition temperatures.

Key Concepts

  • Ignition temperature: The lowest temperature at which a substance catches fire spontaneously
  • Inflammable substances: Have very low ignition temperatures — catch fire easily (e.g., petrol, alcohol, LPG, kerosene)
  • Non-combustible substances: Do not burn (e.g., water, stone, sand)

Remove any one side of the fire triangle to extinguish a fire: cut off fuel, cut off air (CO2 blanket), or lower temperature below ignition point (water).

Types of Combustion

Combustion occurs in different ways depending on the fuel, conditions, and rate of reaction.

  • Rapid Combustion:

    Combustion that occurs very quickly, producing heat and light rapidly. The fuel burns fast when ignited.

    Examples: Burning of LPG on a gas stove, lighting a matchstick, burning of paper.

  • Spontaneous Combustion:

    Combustion that occurs by itself without any external heat source — the substance ignites at room temperature when its ignition temperature is reached naturally.

    Examples: White phosphorus catches fire spontaneously in air at room temperature. Forest fires can start when dry leaves heat up by friction or hot sun. Haystacks in summer.

    This is why white phosphorus is stored underwater.

  • Explosive Combustion:

    Sudden and extremely rapid combustion accompanied by a large amount of heat, light, and a loud sound (explosion). Energy releases faster than it can dissipate.

    Examples: Burning of crackers/fireworks, explosion of gunpowder or dynamite, petrol vapour ignition in engines.

The slow oxidation that occurs in our cells during respiration is NOT combustion — it occurs at body temperature without producing a flame, and releases energy gradually.

Structure of a Flame

A flame is produced when a gas burns. Not all fuels produce a flame — only gases burn with a flame. Solids and liquids must first vaporise before they can burn with a flame.

Three Zones of a Candle Flame

  • Innermost zone (dark/black zone):

    At the base of the flame, around the wick. Contains hot, unburned wax vapour. No combustion occurs here. Coolest part of the flame.

  • Middle zone (luminous/yellow zone):

    Partial combustion occurs. Wax vapour burns but oxygen supply is limited, producing tiny carbon particles that glow yellow/orange. Moderately hot. This is the bright, visible part of the flame.

  • Outer zone (non-luminous/blue zone):

    Complete combustion occurs. Full supply of oxygen allows complete burning to CO2 and water. Hottest part of the flame — used in laboratory and cooking flames.

Observations and Applications

  • Goldsmiths use the outer (non-luminous) zone of the flame for gold work — it is the hottest
  • A blue Bunsen burner flame = complete combustion (outer zone dominant) = very hot and efficient
  • A yellow, sooty flame = incomplete combustion = less efficient; produces soot (carbon particles)
  • Lampblack (soot) from the inner zone is used in printer's ink and black paint

The blue flame of an LPG burner indicates complete combustion — this is why LPG is an efficient fuel with minimal soot.

Types of Fuels and Calorific Value

A fuel is any substance that burns in air to release energy. Fuels exist in solid, liquid, and gaseous states.

Types of Fuels

  • Solid fuels: Wood, coal, charcoal, cow dung cakes, coke. Used for cooking and heating, especially in rural areas.
  • Liquid fuels: Petrol, diesel, kerosene, alcohol. Used in vehicles, lamps, and stoves. Easy to store and transport.
  • Gaseous fuels: LPG (liquefied petroleum gas), CNG (compressed natural gas), coal gas, biogas. Most efficient and cleanest burning fuels.

Calorific Value

The amount of heat energy produced when 1 kg of a fuel is completely burned. Unit: kilojoules per kilogram (kJ/kg).

  • Wood: ~17,000 kJ/kg
  • Coal: ~25,000–33,000 kJ/kg
  • Petrol: ~45,000 kJ/kg
  • LPG: ~55,000 kJ/kg
  • CNG (methane): ~50,000 kJ/kg
  • Hydrogen: ~150,000 kJ/kg (highest!)

An ideal fuel: high calorific value, low cost, readily available, easy to store, produces no harmful gases.

Harmful Effects of Burning Fuels

While fuels provide energy, burning them releases pollutants that harm health and the environment.

Pollutants Produced

  • Carbon dioxide (CO2): Released by all fossil fuel combustion. Major greenhouse gas causing global warming and climate change. Oceans absorb CO2 → ocean acidification.
  • Carbon monoxide (CO): Produced during incomplete combustion (insufficient oxygen). Highly toxic — binds to haemoglobin, preventing oxygen transport in blood. Odourless and colourless — "the silent killer." Caused by faulty burners, poorly ventilated rooms.
  • Sulphur dioxide (SO2): From burning coal and diesel (containing sulphur). Causes acid rain (SO2 + H2O → H2SO4), damages lungs, kills plants.
  • Nitrogen oxides (NOx): Formed at high combustion temperatures. Cause smog, acid rain, and respiratory problems.
  • Particulate matter / Soot: Tiny carbon particles from incomplete combustion. Cause respiratory diseases, reduce visibility.

Acid Rain

When SO2 and NOx dissolve in rainwater, they form sulphuric acid (H2SO4) and nitric acid (HNO3), making rain acidic.

SO2 + H2O → H2SO3 (sulphurous acid)
2SO2 + O2 + 2H2O → 2H2SO4 (sulphuric acid)

Effects of acid rain:

  • Damages historic monuments (e.g., Taj Mahal — marble cancer)
  • Kills aquatic life in lakes and rivers
  • Destroys forests and crops
  • Corrodes metal structures

Catalytic converters in cars convert harmful CO and NOx into less harmful CO2 and N2 before exhaust is released.

Chapter Summary

Key Concepts

  • Combustion requires fuel + oxygen + heat (ignition temperature)
  • 3 types: rapid, spontaneous, explosive combustion
  • Flame has 3 zones: inner (dark), middle (luminous), outer (hottest, blue)
  • Calorific value = heat released per kg of fuel burned
  • Incomplete combustion produces CO (toxic) and soot
  • Burning fossil fuels releases CO2, SO2, NOx — causing climate change and acid rain

Key Terms

  • Combustion: Chemical reaction of fuel with oxygen releasing heat and light
  • Ignition temperature: Minimum temperature needed to start combustion
  • Calorific value: Heat energy per kg of fuel (kJ/kg)
  • Inflammable: Catches fire very easily (low ignition temperature)
  • Acid rain: Rain made acidic by SO2 and NOx pollution
  • Global warming: Rise in Earth's temperature due to CO2 and other greenhouse gases
  • Luminous flame: Bright yellow flame from partial combustion

Fire is one of humanity's oldest tools — understanding the chemistry of combustion helps us harness its power safely and minimise its harmful impact on our world.
