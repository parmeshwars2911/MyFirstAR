Synthetic Fibres and Plastics

Chapter 3 · Grade 8 Science

Fibres make up the fabrics we wear every day. While some fibres come from nature (cotton, wool, silk), many modern materials are made in laboratories from chemicals — these are called synthetic fibres.

Topics covered: Natural vs synthetic fibres · Types of synthetic fibres · Plastics · Environmental impact · 4Rs

Natural vs Synthetic Fibres

Fibres are long, thin, thread-like structures used to make fabrics and other materials.

Natural Fibres

Obtained directly from plants or animals

  • Plant-based: Cotton (from cotton plant), Jute (from jute plant), Linen
  • Animal-based: Wool (from sheep/goat), Silk (from silkworm cocoons)

Advantages: Biodegradable, comfortable, breathable

Disadvantages: Expensive, wrinkle easily, require more care

Synthetic Fibres

Man-made from chemical compounds derived from petroleum (petrochemicals)

  • Made of repeating units called monomers linked into long chains called polymers
  • Examples: Rayon, Nylon, Polyester, Acrylic

Advantages: Cheap, durable, easy to wash, quick-drying

Disadvantages: Non-biodegradable, trap heat, can melt near flame

The word polymer comes from Greek: "poly" = many, "mer" = unit. Cotton, silk, and wool are also natural polymers!

Types of Synthetic Fibres

  • Rayon (Artificial Silk):
    • Made from wood pulp (cellulose) — a semi-synthetic fibre
    • Resembles silk in appearance and feel, but much cheaper
    • Used for: sarees, blouses, carpets, bed sheets
    • Can be woven like silk and dyed in many colours
  • Nylon:
    • First fully synthetic fibre, made from coal, water, and air
    • Very strong, lustrous, elastic, and lightweight; does not absorb water
    • Used for: toothbrush bristles, stockings, ropes, parachutes, seat belts, sleeping bags
  • Polyester (PET — Polyethylene Terephthalate):
    • Made from repeating units of ester; very durable, wrinkle-resistant
    • Used for: clothing (polyester shirts/blouses), plastic bottles, audio/video tapes, sails
    • Terylene is a common polyester fabric
  • Acrylic (Artificial Wool):
    • Resembles wool; lightweight, soft, warm
    • Used for: sweaters, blankets, shawls, carpets
    • Cheaper than natural wool and resistant to moths

Properties of Synthetic Fibres

Synthetic fibres have several properties that make them highly popular in everyday use.

Advantages

  • Strong and durable: Nylon is stronger than steel wire of the same thickness
  • Lightweight: Comfortable to wear and easy to carry
  • Easy to wash: Most are machine washable and dry quickly
  • Wrinkle-resistant: Retain shape after washing
  • Less expensive: More affordable than silk or wool
  • Wide colour range: Can be dyed in vivid, long-lasting colours
  • Elastic: Stretch and return to original shape

Disadvantages

  • Catch fire easily: Melt on heating and can cause severe burns — do not wear synthetic clothes near a flame
  • Poor moisture absorption: Can feel uncomfortable in hot weather
  • Non-biodegradable: Persist in environment for hundreds of years
  • Micro-plastic pollution: Washing releases tiny plastic fibres into waterways
  • Static electricity: Build up static charge easily

Lab coats and uniforms worn near fire must be made of cotton, not synthetic fabric.

Plastics

Plastics are synthetic polymers that can be moulded into any shape. They are made from petrochemicals and are extremely versatile.

Thermoplastics

Soften on heating; can be reshaped and remoulded multiple times

  • PVC (Polyvinyl chloride): Pipes, electrical insulation, flooring
  • Polythene: Carry bags, plastic bottles, food packaging
  • Polystyrene: Foam cups, packaging material
  • Nylon: Gears, machinery parts

Can be recycled

Thermosetting Plastics

Set permanently on heating; cannot be remoulded once hardened

  • Bakelite: Electrical switches, handles, plugs — does not conduct electricity or heat
  • Melamine: Floor tiles, kitchenware, fire-resistant uniforms
  • Urea-formaldehyde: Adhesives, buttons

Cannot be recycled easily

Bakelite was the world's first fully synthetic plastic, invented in 1907 by Leo Baekeland.

Plastics and the Environment

Plastics are enormously useful but pose serious environmental threats because they are non-biodegradable — they do not break down naturally for hundreds to thousands of years.

Environmental Problems

  • Clog drains and sewers, causing floods
  • Kill wildlife: animals mistake plastic for food
  • Marine pollution: ocean plastic harms marine ecosystems
  • Burning plastic releases toxic gases (dioxins, furans)
  • Microplastics enter food chains and drinking water
  • Soil pollution makes land infertile

The 4Rs of Plastic Management

  • Reduce: Use less plastic — carry cloth or jute bags instead of plastic bags
  • Reuse: Use plastic containers multiple times before discarding
  • Recycle: Collect and process plastic waste into new products. Recycled plastics are marked with recycling codes (1–7).
  • Recover: Extract energy or materials from plastic waste when recycling is not possible

Many states in India have banned single-use plastics. Using biodegradable alternatives is the need of the hour.

Chapter Summary

Key Concepts

  • Synthetic fibres are man-made polymers derived from petroleum
  • 4 main synthetic fibres: Rayon, Nylon, Polyester, Acrylic
  • Synthetic fibres: strong, cheap, easy care — but flammable and non-biodegradable
  • Thermoplastics can be remoulded; thermosetting plastics cannot
  • Plastics are non-biodegradable — major environmental pollutants
  • 4Rs: Reduce, Reuse, Recycle, Recover

Key Terms

  • Polymer: Large molecule made of repeating monomer units
  • Monomer: Small repeating unit of a polymer
  • Rayon: Semi-synthetic fibre from wood pulp
  • Nylon: First fully synthetic fibre
  • Bakelite: First fully synthetic plastic (thermosetting)
  • PET: Polyethylene terephthalate (common polyester)
  • Non-biodegradable: Cannot be broken down naturally

Synthetic materials have transformed modern life, but responsible use and disposal are essential to protect our planet.
