Perfect Squares

A perfect square (or square number) is an integer that can be expressed as the square of another integer. In other words, it is the product of an integer multiplied by itself.

The first 15 perfect squares are:

Properties of Perfect Squares

  • The square of any integer is always non-negative (zero or positive). For example, (−5)² = 25 > 0.
  • Numbers ending in digits 2, 3, 7, or 8 can never be perfect squares. For example, 72, 43, 57, 98 are not perfect squares.
  • Perfect squares can only end in digits: 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9.
  • If a perfect square ends in 1, its square root ends in 1 or 9.
  • If a perfect square ends in 4, its square root ends in 2 or 8.
  • If a perfect square ends in 9, its square root ends in 3 or 7.
  • If a perfect square ends in 5, its square root must end in 5.
  • If a perfect square ends in 6, its square root ends in 4 or 6.
  • A perfect square always has an odd number of zeros at the end... actually, perfect squares end in an even number of zeros (e.g., 100, 10000).
  • Perfect squares of even numbers are even; perfect squares of odd numbers are odd.

Patterns in Squares and Pythagorean Triplets

Pattern 1: Difference of Consecutive Perfect Squares

The difference between two consecutive perfect squares n² and (n+1)² is always an odd number:

(n + 1)² − n² = n² + 2n + 1 − n² = 2n + 1

Examples: 4 − 1 = 3,   9 − 4 = 5,   16 − 9 = 7,   25 − 16 = 9,   36 − 25 = 11,   49 − 36 = 13

This means: 1 + 3 + 5 + 7 + ... + (2n−1) = n²   (sum of first n odd numbers = n²)

Pattern 2: Expressing a Square as a Sum of Odd Numbers

1 = 1,  4 = 1+3,  9 = 1+3+5,  16 = 1+3+5+7,  25 = 1+3+5+7+9

Pythagorean Triplets

A Pythagorean triplet is a set of three positive integers (a, b, c) such that a² + b² = c². Here, c is the hypotenuse of a right-angled triangle.

a² + b² = c²

Generating Pythagorean Triplets

For any integer m > 1, the three numbers (2m), (m² – 1), and (m² + 1) form a Pythagorean triplet:

(2m)² + (m² – 1)² = (m² + 1)²

Finding Square Root by Prime Factorisation

The prime factorisation method works well for perfect squares and numbers that are products of small primes. The key idea is that in a perfect square, every prime factor appears an even number of times (in pairs).

Method

  1. Find the prime factorisation of the given number.
  2. Arrange the prime factors in pairs of identical primes.
  3. Take one factor from each pair.
  4. Multiply all the selected factors together to get the square root.

Example 1: Find the square root of 324

Step 1: Prime factorise 324:   324 = 2 × 162 = 2 × 2 × 81 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 27 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 9 = 2 × 2 × 3 × 3 × 3 × 3

324 = 2² × 3² × 3² = (2 × 3 × 3)²

Step 2: Pair up: (2 × 2) × (3 × 3) × (3 × 3)

Step 3: Take one from each pair: 2 × 3 × 3 = 18

Therefore, √324 = 18. Check: 18² = 324. Correct.

Example 2: Find the square root of 1764

1764 = 2 × 882 = 2 × 2 × 441 = 4 × 441 = 4 × 9 × 49 = 2² × 3² × 7²

√1764 = √(2² × 3² × 7²) = 2 × 3 × 7 = 42

Check: 42² = 1764. Correct.

Checking if a Number is a Perfect Square

Factorise the number. If every prime factor appears an even number of times, it is a perfect square. If any prime factor appears an odd number of times, it is NOT a perfect square.

Example: Is 252 a perfect square? 252 = 2² × 3² × 7. The factor 7 appears only once (odd). So 252 is NOT a perfect square. The smallest number to multiply 252 by to make it a perfect square is 7 (giving 252 × 7 = 1764 = 42²).

Finding Square Root by Long Division Method

The long division method is used for larger numbers and also gives non-integer square roots to any number of decimal places. It works even when prime factorisation is difficult.

Method — Steps

  1. Group the digits of the number in pairs, starting from the units place and moving left. Put a bar over each pair. If the number of digits is odd, the leftmost group has only one digit.
  2. Find the largest integer whose square is less than or equal to the leftmost group. Write it as the first digit of the quotient and also as the divisor. Subtract its square from the first group and bring down the next pair.
  3. Double the current quotient and write it as the new partial divisor (with a blank at the units place). Find the largest digit d such that (new divisor with d at units place) × d ≤ current remainder. Write d in the quotient and complete the division step.
  4. Repeat step 3 by bringing down the next pair each time until all pairs have been used.

Worked Example: Find √529

Group digits: 5 | 29 (two groups)

  • Largest square ≤ 5 is 4 = 2². Quotient so far: 2. Remainder = 5 − 4 = 1. Bring down 29. Current dividend = 129.
  • Double quotient: 2 × 2 = 4. New partial divisor = 4_. Find d: 42 × 2 = 84 (too small?), 43 × 3 = 129. So d = 3, new divisor = 43, remainder = 129 − 129 = 0.
√529 = 23   (check: 23² = 529)

Worked Example: Find √1024

Group digits: 10 | 24 (two groups)

  • Largest square ≤ 10 is 9 = 3². Quotient: 3. Remainder = 10 − 9 = 1. Bring down 24. Dividend = 124.
  • Double quotient: 3 × 2 = 6. New partial divisor = 6_. Find d: 62 × 2 = 124. So d = 2, remainder = 124 − 124 = 0.
√1024 = 32   (check: 32² = 1024)

Note: When the remainder is 0 and all digit pairs have been used, the number is a perfect square. If there is still a remainder, you can add pairs of zeros after a decimal point and continue the process to get decimal digits of the square root.

Square Roots of Decimals and Fractions

Square Roots of Fractions

For a fraction p/q (where both p and q are perfect squares), the square root is found by taking the square root of the numerator and denominator separately:

√(p/q) = √p / √q

Examples:

  • √(25/49) = √25 / √49 = 5/7
  • √(16/81) = √16 / √81 = 4/9   (check: (4/9)² = 16/81)
  • √(9/144) = √9 / √144 = 3/12 = 1/4   (simplify the result)
  • √(1/4) = 1/2   (since (1/2)² = 1/4)

Square Roots of Decimals

Method 1 — Convert to a fraction: Express the decimal as a fraction, simplify if possible, then apply the fraction rule above.

Example: √(0.09) = √(9/100) = √9 / √100 = 3/10 = 0.3

Example: √(0.0625) = √(625/10000) = 25/100 = 0.25

Method 2 — Long division for decimals: Use the long division method directly on the decimal number. Group digits in pairs on both sides of the decimal point — pairs to the left of the decimal start from the decimal and move left; pairs to the right start from the decimal and move right.

√(2.25): Groups are 2 | .25 → √2.25 = 1.5 (since 1.5² = 2.25)

General Rule

  • If a decimal has 2 decimal places, its square root has 1 decimal place.
  • If a decimal has 4 decimal places, its square root has 2 decimal places.
  • The number of decimal places in the square root is always half the number of decimal places in the original number (for perfect square decimals).

More Decimal Examples

  • √(1.44) = 1.2
  • √(0.01) = 0.1
  • √(0.0016) = 0.04
  • √(12.25) = 3.5

More Fraction Examples

  • √(4/9) = 2/3
  • √(49/64) = 7/8
  • √(100/121) = 10/11
  • √(36/169) = 6/13

Chapter 6 Summary – Squares and Square Roots

Key Properties

  • Perfect squares end in 0, 1, 4, 5, 6, or 9 — never in 2, 3, 7, or 8.
  • Square of any integer is non-negative.
  • In the prime factorisation of a perfect square, every prime factor appears an even number of times.
  • (n+1)² − n² = 2n + 1 (odd number)
  • Sum of first n odd numbers = n²

Pythagorean Triplets Formula

For m > 1: (2m), (m²–1), (m²+1)

Known triplets: (3,4,5), (5,12,13), (8,15,17), (6,8,10)

Key Formulas

√(p/q) = √p / √q
(n + 1)² − n² = 2n + 1

Methods for Finding Square Root

Prime Factorisation: Factorise → pair up factors → take one from each pair → multiply selected factors.

Long Division: Group digits in pairs from right → find first quotient → double quotient to get new divisor → find next digit → repeat.

Decimal Rule

  • √(0.09) = 0.3 (2 decimal places → 1)
  • √(0.0625) = 0.25 (4 decimal places → 2)

Quick Check: After finding a square root, always verify by squaring your answer. A number is a perfect square only if all prime factors appear in pairs.
